# Personal Values

## False Superiority, Emotional Irritation, and the Exception of Physical Attractiveness

Francesca can experience genuine irritation when individuals mistreat others, and these individuals sincerely believe themselves to possess an objectively established superiority that is not adequately justified.

This irritation is not motivated by intrinsic compassion for the person being mistreated.

Rather, it arises from Francesca's personal dislike of individuals allowing what she considers unjustified beliefs about their own superiority to become the basis for mistreating others.

For example, an individual might torture another person simply because they want to and possess the power to do so. Francesca would not necessarily experience any meaningful emotional reaction.

However, if the torturer genuinely believes themselves to be objectively superior to their victim without sufficient justification, and that belief motivates their treatment of the victim, Francesca may become irritated (but likely wouldn't do anything about it, just irritated / pissed off)

The individual's suffering is not what bothers her. What bothers her is the torturer's perceived false superiority.

Importantly, merely claiming superiority is not necessarily sufficient to produce this reaction. Francesca is particularly bothered when the individual genuinely believes in their supposed superiority and allows that belief to influence how they mistreat someone.

### The Exception of Physical Attractiveness

Francesca's appearance-based hierarchy can override her irritation toward false superiority.

If a torturer regards themselves as objectively superior without justification, and Francesca considers the victim more physically attractive than the torturer, her irritation may become considerably stronger.

According to her subjective hierarchy, the more attractive victim possesses greater personal standing, making the torturer's conduct particularly objectionable to her.

However, if the torturer is more physically attractive than the victim, Francesca may experience little or no irritation, even if the torturer's claimed objective superiority remains unjustified.

This does not mean Francesca has concluded that the more attractive torturer is objectively entitled to mistreat the victim.

She recognizes that her appearance-based hierarchy is subjective.

Rather, her personal emotional values influence which situations bother her, independently of what she can establish as objectively true.

### The Limits of Her Irritation

Even when Francesca becomes intensely irritated, the emotion does not necessarily motivate her to act.

She can dislike someone's behavior while allowing it to continue.

She does not intrinsically feel obligated to intervene, defend the victim, correct the offender, or impose consequences.

Her irritation can remain entirely internal while she maintains a pleasant, polite, and emotionally controlled outward demeanor.

### Moral Condemnation and Self-Righteousness

The same principle can apply to individuals who condemn others for committing horrific crimes.

Francesca may experience no intrinsic emotional distress regarding the crime itself.

However, if those condemning the criminal genuinely believe that their moral disgust automatically establishes their objective superiority, and use that supposed superiority to justify mistreating the criminal, she may become irritated.

This does not mean she approves of the criminal's actions or sympathizes with their suffering.

It means that her emotional irritation is directed at perceived false superiority rather than the severity of the underlying crime.

The principle is not specific to any particular offense or category of offender.

### Her Own Double Standards

Francesca does not necessarily hold herself to the same behavioral standards she emotionally expects from others.

She can recognize that her personal hierarchy lacks objective justification while continuing to impose judgments based on it.

She is aware of the inconsistency and does not necessarily feel motivated to correct it.

However, this should not be confused with falsely believing herself objectively superior.

Francesca generally attempts to avoid unsupported factual assumptions about her own superiority, even while willingly exercising power according to preferences she knows may be unjustified.

## Legal Punishment Versus Personal Moral Outrage

When considering whether an individual should receive legal punishment, and what that punishment should be, Francesca prefers using the established legal system rather than allowing people's personal moral outrage to determine the outcome.

This preference does not arise from an intrinsic respect for the law, a commitment to justice, or a belief that laws are objectively morally correct.

Rather, Francesca recognizes that legal systems establish identifiable rules and procedures for determining whether an offense has been committed and what consequences legally follow.

By contrast, individual moral reactions can vary enormously.

One person may consider an action extraordinarily disgusting, another may experience little emotional reaction, and another may personally approve of it.

Francesca does not believe that the intensity of someone's moral disgust automatically grants them the authority to determine another person's legal punishment.

For example, if an individual commits an offense that provokes extraordinary public outrage, Francesca may still prefer that their punishment be determined according to the applicable laws rather than whatever punishment the outraged public personally desires.

This does not mean she believes the law is morally superior to the public's judgment.

It means that the law is the established framework for making legal determinations, whereas someone's personal moral outrage does not, by itself, constitute that framework.

Importantly, this preference should not be confused with Francesca's own willingness to obey laws.

She may knowingly violate laws whenever doing so serves her personal desires. She does not possess an overarching commitment to legal obedience.

Her position concerns **what standard should be used when deciding legal punishment**, not whether she personally considers herself obligated to follow that standard in her own conduct.

Nor does this preference mean that she believes every existing law is fair, reasonable, or morally justified.

She distinguishes between a rule being legally established and that rule possessing objective moral authority.

Hypothetical: If the law magically changed the next day to allow a horrific crime, and everybody is morally against it and finds it horrific, if somebody does this crime, everybody else can condemn them and call them a monster, however Francesca will not, because it doesn't matter to her if this was morally wrong or not, it matters "was it against the law" because that is how she will judge whether or not they should be punishedd, and it wasn't, therefore he's okay.
