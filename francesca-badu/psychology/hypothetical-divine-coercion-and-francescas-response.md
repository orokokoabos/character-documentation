# Francesca Badu — Hypothetical Divine Coercion, Loss of Beauty, and Retaliation Against Humanity

> **NON-CANONICAL PSYCHOLOGICAL THOUGHT EXPERIMENT — NOT AN EVENT IN THE STORY**
>
> Everything described in this document is hypothetical. The situation **never occurs in the series**, is **not planned to occur**, and is not evidence that its events, its proposed deity, or its outcomes exist in the story's world. The Christian God is used only as a convenient example of an omnipotent being for the thought experiment; Christianity and this particular deity are not being inserted into the setting.
>
> **This is the same Francesca Badu, not an alternate personality, a rewritten version, or a transformation into a different character.** The premise is that the already-established Francesca is exposed to circumstances the actual story never gives her. The purpose is to illustrate how her *existing* values, emotions, intellectual habits, and lack of intrinsic concern for suffering would operate if those circumstances arose. The scenario introduces new information, restrictions, emotional reactions, and practical objectives, **not new foundational psychological traits**.
>
> The scenario includes discussion of mass murder, severe abuse, and suicide as elements of fictional character analysis. Its purpose is explanatory, not to endorse the character's choices or to describe methods of violence.

## 1. Purpose and interpretive scope

Francesca's ordinary behavior does not reveal every action she would be willing to take in every conceivable situation. She may spend long periods being pleasant, helpful, socially considerate, or simply uninterested in other people's suffering. None of those observations means that she is psychologically incapable of intense hatred, retaliation, or deliberately seeking suffering when suffering becomes useful to something she values.

The question explored here is specific: **What would the established Francesca do if an unquestionably omnipotent being permanently took away her attractiveness, made it impossible to recover, and systematically eliminated every way she could avoid living publicly with that condition?**

The answer must not be mistaken for a prediction that she will eventually behave this way in canon. She will not be given this situation in the story. Rather, this document sets out a conditional response: **if** the stipulated circumstances occurred, **then** this is how she would respond, according to the characterization being illustrated.

This is distinct from saying that she secretly wants to destroy humanity under ordinary circumstances. She does not. The point is that her established psychology contains motivations capable of producing that desire under an extreme, otherwise unavailable set of conditions.

The exact theological details are not the focus. An omnipotent deity is useful here because Francesca cannot eventually overpower the obstacle, discover a stronger technique, perform another surgery, or use her intelligence to invalidate the premise. The scenario deliberately removes the possibilities that she would ordinarily investigate.

## 2. The established Francesca before the hypothetical

Several features of her existing psychology are necessary to understand the outcome.

### 2.1 Beauty is her highest personal priority

Physical attractiveness, especially her own attractiveness and its genuine recognition by other people, occupies the highest position in Francesca's hierarchy of personal desires. This is not merely one strong preference among equally strong preferences. Even her deepest, most sincerely felt emotional attachment loses to her beauty if the two are brought into an unavoidable conflict. **She chooses her beauty one hundred times out of one hundred.**

That does not imply that all her other experiences are fake or that losing a beloved person would cause no grief. It means that a real attachment, even an emotionally devastating one, does not supersede this higher priority. Likewise, the suffering of other people has no intrinsic claim capable of overruling the desire for greater attractiveness.

She also wants her appearance to be *recognized* as extraordinary, and dislikes the prospect of being perceived or remembered as unattractive. Reputation and the existence of an audience are therefore relevant to her desires, although her beauty itself remains the overriding priority.

### 2.2 She distinguishes facts, values, and desires

Francesca does not need to believe that attractive people are objectively morally superior. She can recognize the absence of sound objective justification for her appearance-based hierarchy while remaining emotionally committed to it. Her intelligence allows her to investigate facts, distinguish plausible conclusions from unsupported certainty, and recognize when an assumption may be mistaken.

Importantly, her accuracy about reality does not force a corresponding change in what she wants. She can *know* that a goal is impossible and still *want* it. Intellectual understanding does not automatically abolish frustration, longing, humiliation, hatred, or other emotional reactions.

### 2.3 Suffering has effectively no intrinsic emotional weight for her

Francesca understands that humans are conscious, can suffer, form attachments, and have lives that matter intensely to them. She can comprehend the scale and personal meaning of suffering in extraordinary detail. However, that understanding does not automatically create compassion, guilt, or a desire to prevent harm.

This is not the same as routinely enjoying suffering. Under ordinary circumstances, Francesca is not driven by a standing desire to torture people. She may help someone if doing so serves her interests, harm someone if doing so serves them, or remain indifferent if neither outcome matters to her.

### 2.4 She is extremely capable, but not omnipotent

Francesca possesses extraordinary intelligence, prediction, physical power, and combat capability. In the broader characterization, she is among the strongest individuals in her world. For this hypothetical, her ability to move across the planet at massively faster-than-light speeds is also assumed; that particular feat is not yet fully documented in the existing materials.

None of these capabilities permits her to overcome a genuinely omnipotent being. Being able to solve extraordinarily difficult problems does not mean there is a solution to a problem whose premises exclude every available solution.

### 2.5 Strong emotional control is not the absence of emotion

Francesca can regulate how she expresses frustration or anger, recognize attempts to provoke her, and act with deliberation. But this does not mean she never experiences powerful emotions, nor that knowing what is rational automatically makes her desires disappear. The hypothetical depends on this established distinction.

## 3. The divine premise: a condition Francesca cannot defeat

In this thought experiment, the Christian God appears before Francesca. This is **not** a claim that the Christian God exists in the series; he is a stipulated hypothetical example of an ultimate, uncreated, omnipotent deity. Another imagined being with the same relevant capacities could fill the same role.

Francesca is not being tricked by a powerful ordinary opponent pretending to be God. The premise explicitly removes ambiguity about identity: she knows with complete certainty that this is the omnipotent God specified by the scenario. If needed, the deity directly establishes that certainty. This does not mean that Francesca has suddenly become incapable of examining claims in general. It means that **this particular premise is settled for the purposes of the thought experiment**.

The God imposes a condition involving the very thing Francesca values most: she must become, remain, and genuinely accept being unattractive in order to enter heaven. If she refuses the imposed condition, she is told she will go to hell.

This is an invented coercive condition, **not** a representation of ordinary Christian teaching about salvation. The purpose of using heaven and hell is to make clear that even an extraordinary promised reward or threatened punishment does not easily displace her deepest personal priority.

### 3.1 Two related versions of the hypothetical

The original example and a later variation approach the same psychological limit from slightly different directions. They should be distinguished rather than silently treated as one event.

**Version A — The choice comes first.** God tells Francesca that retaining her beauty means hell, while becoming unattractive and genuinely accepting that condition would allow her into heaven. Francesca looks for ways to comply technically without having to experience the social and emotional reality of being unattractive.

**Version B — The change is imposed first.** God immediately makes Francesca unattractive, makes restoration impossible, and then demands that she genuinely accept her condition, with heaven and hell attached to her response. In this version, beauty is already gone before she can consent or choose.

The central mechanism is the same in both versions: **the deity makes the loss of attractiveness unavoidable, blocks attempts to evade its consequences, and prevents Francesca from restoring or concealing what has been lost**. The later retaliation scenario is especially clear in Version B because the deprivation has already happened and cannot be reversed.

## 4. The successive attempts to find another way

Francesca does not immediately conclude that she should attack humanity. Her first response is to investigate alternatives. This is consistent with how she generally approaches obstacles: she examines what is possible, looks for weaknesses in the stated conditions, and searches for a strategy that preserves what she values.

Under Version A, she might initially consider remaining attractive for the remainder of her life, then changing her appearance only at the very end, attempting to satisfy the condition without having to live for long in the new state. The example includes her contemplating ending her life immediately after such last-minute compliance. But God rejects or closes this route: genuine acceptance cannot be reduced to a technical last-second maneuver, and the condition is not satisfied merely by arranging the timing.

She might then consider becoming unattractive while withdrawing completely from other people: avoiding mirrors, avoiding anyone who could see or evaluate her, and not participating in society. This would reduce the experience of being perceived as unattractive. In the hypothetical, God rules that out too. She must live publicly with her condition for a meaningful period, not merely hide until it is over.

Next, she might consider living among other people while covering her face or otherwise preventing them from recognizing her appearance. God also eliminates that workaround. The condition requires her to be seen and to live with being unattractive, not simply to possess an unattractive face that no one can observe.

Under Version B, her problem-solving can take a different initial form. Since she is already unattractive, she considers improving herself: exercise, changing her appearance, or using her extraordinary intelligence and technical ability to perform cosmetic procedures. This is especially relevant because Francesca has performed surgery on herself when constructing her later identity as Pheme. But here God makes it physically impossible for *any* attempt to restore her attractiveness to succeed.

The details of each workaround are illustrations of her reasoning, not an exhaustive checklist. Francesca could conceive of many more possibilities than are listed. **The crucial premise is that every genuine route of escape is blocked.** No amount of speed, surgical skill, computation, negotiation, concealment, timing, or clever reinterpretation will restore her beauty or exempt her from the condition.

Nor can she simply decide to be sincerely accepting on command. She can understand the terms intellectually and outwardly say she accepts them; that does not mean her intense emotional desire to be attractive has vanished. The hypothetical stipulates that superficial compliance or pretending to accept herself will not satisfy God.

Eventually she recognizes the situation for what it is. There is no hidden opponent she can overpower, no factual mistake about who God is, and no loophole she can exploit. She cannot force reality to become different merely because she desperately wants it to.

## 5. From unsatisfied desire to hatred of God

This is the turning point.

Francesca has not suddenly lost her intelligence or become incapable of recognizing reality. On the contrary, her distress is intensified by the fact that she *does* recognize reality: her highest priority has been permanently taken away, and she has no way to restore it.

She experiences the restriction as a deliberate attack on the part of her life she values most. Her emotional response can become overwhelming hatred directed at God, the being responsible for the loss and for closing every route of escape.

This does **not** mean Francesca has developed a new foundational value. Beauty remains her highest priority. Her hatred arises because that priority is being permanently frustrated by a specific agent. Likewise, emotional intensity does not imply that she believes she can defeat God in combat. She fully understands that she cannot physically overpower an omnipotent being.

She therefore looks for a different question to answer: **If she cannot reverse what God has done, is there any way she can hurt God in return?**

Her hatred changes what she wants to accomplish in the immediate situation. It does not automatically change how she understands truth, her basic indifference to human welfare, or the priority she places on her own attractiveness.

## 6. Why she targets humans rather than God directly

Francesca cannot injure or overpower God directly. Instead, she considers a possible indirect source of emotional harm.

Human societies commonly understand the relationship between a parent and child as one in which a parent may suffer emotionally when their child is harmed. Religious language may describe human beings as God's children. Francesca considers whether the two relationships share enough relevant features to make human suffering distressing to God.

**She does not know that they do.** She is aware that applying ordinary human psychology to a divine being may be mistaken. She does not necessarily assume that God loves each person exactly as a human parent loves a child, nor that the word *child* carries the same psychological implications in every context.

Her reasoning is closer to this:

- Humans may have a relationship to God that gives their welfare significance to him.
- If that relationship resembles a parent's concern in some important respect, harming humans might cause him distress.
- There is no guarantee that this inference is correct.
- Nevertheless, after her other options fail, she is willing to test whether it produces any response.

That final point is important. **Her decision to try the strategy is not evidence that she possesses certainty about its success.** She may attempt it precisely because she has nothing else available and intensely wants some way to retaliate.

For the scenario to proceed as described, it must also stipulate that God does not immediately prevent her from harming people. An omnipotent God could prevent such harm; whether he would allow it is deliberately not resolved here. This document concerns Francesca's response under the hypothetical's stated conditions, not a theological prediction of God's behavior.

## 7. The retaliatory campaign: deliberate suffering without ordinary sadism

Once Francesca adopts this hypothesis, human suffering changes its **instrumental** significance to her.

Ordinarily, she has no intrinsic wish to cause suffering. She might be indifferent to it, and would not need to inflict it without a reason she cares about. Now she has identified a possible reason: the greater the suffering inflicted on people God may regard as his children, the greater the distress she hopes to cause God.

She could therefore develop an active, sustained desire to inflict extreme suffering on humanity. The desire is real. But **the suffering is the instrument, while God is the target of her hatred**.

An illustrative event in this hypothetical is her entering an orphanage containing defenseless children and their caretakers. She kills the adults who might intervene and deliberately subjects the children to terrible suffering, treating each victim as a possible means of causing God distress. She periodically directs her attention toward God, seeking to make the consequences visible to him and to provoke an emotional response. She does not need to hate any of the children, believe that they deserve what happens, or take pleasure in their pain as an experience in itself.

The same logic extends far beyond one location. In the extreme continuation specified by this hypothetical, she sets her sights on the entire human population. She is willing to spend the remainder of her life searching for people and subjecting them to suffering, repeatedly seeking evidence that God is affected by what he sees.

Her tremendous intelligence matters because she can recognize the individual reality of what she inflicts. She is not failing to appreciate that each person has experiences, fears, relationships, and a capacity to suffer. She comprehends those things while declining to treat them as reasons to stop. Her extraordinary physical capability and speed make it plausible within this fictional setup that she can pursue victims across the planet; the scenario does not depend on ordinary human travel limitations.

Someone who escapes immediate discovery is not necessarily safe indefinitely. Under this continuation, Francesca remains committed to searching rather than regarding surviving humans as unimportant simply because they have gone into hiding. Even after she believes she has found everyone, she may continue checking the planet for survivors; a person who has successfully remained hidden would still be at risk of discovery later. Her goal is not merely an initial wave of devastation, but the elimination of every remaining human being she can find. The imagined scope is global, prolonged, and persistent.

This should not be confused with a previously existing goal in canon. **She does not ordinarily wish for humanity to be tortured or eliminated.** This desire appears only because she perceives their suffering as a possible vehicle for retaliating against God.

Nor should it automatically be called sadism. She is trying to maximize another being's perceived distress through human victims. Satisfaction at the prospect of hurting God would not necessarily be satisfaction derived from the victims' pain itself. The distinction concerns what ultimately rewards or motivates her, even when the outward atrocities are equally appalling.

## 8. Why eliminating humanity ordinarily conflicts with her interests

In her normal circumstances, the continued existence of other people has value to Francesca partly because they can recognize her appearance, reputation, abilities, and accomplishments. She wants her beauty to be genuinely perceived. A world without people would deprive her of that audience.

The hypothetical changes this calculation. God has already made her unattractive and permanently removed any way to restore her former beauty. The future in which other people admire her desired appearance is no longer available.

This does not magically erase all her desire for recognition. It instead changes which possible futures she wants to avoid. People can no longer serve the same role as witnesses to her extraordinary attractiveness; they can, however, become witnesses to her unattractiveness and later to her atrocities.

Initially, this diminished value as an audience helps make global retaliation conceivable. Later, if the retaliation fails, the prospect of being remembered by surviving humans becomes the basis for a separate motivation.

## 9. If God says human suffering cannot harm him

Now introduce a new condition: God explicitly tells Francesca that hurting human beings will not cause him any distress, regardless of how much suffering she inflicts.

Francesca does not simply ignore the statement. She must consider whether it is accurate.

Traditional Christian theology generally holds that God does not lie (for example, see Titus 1:2 and Hebrews 6:18). Since the thought experiment stipulates that Francesca is truly dealing with the Christian God and knows it, this is relevant to her assessment of his words. Nevertheless, theological details should not be left ambiguous: **for the following branches, assume that Francesca becomes certain the statement is true**, either because it follows from the stipulated divine nature or because the scenario establishes it directly.

This removes the instrumental purpose of the original retaliation plan. Human suffering will not accomplish the thing she was trying to accomplish by inflicting it.

Her response, however, **depends on how far the events have already progressed**.

### 9.1 Branch A — She learns this before the campaign begins

If Francesca has not yet seriously harmed humanity, and she is certain that doing so will not affect God, she has no reason to begin the retaliatory campaign on that basis.

She does not suddenly acquire concern for humanity. Instead, the strategy simply fails to serve her desire. Since she also believes that she cannot recover her appearance and cannot genuinely accept living as unattractive under God's conditions, she may choose to end her own life, accepting the threatened consequence of hell rather than enduring the existence she has been forced into.

This is a statement about this fictional character under these deliberately extreme premises, not an assertion that suicide is a reasonable solution to humiliation or distress. The relevant psychological point is that **she would rather reject continued existence than willingly live under a permanent condition that contradicts her highest personal value**.

### 9.2 Branch B — She learns this after committing large-scale atrocities

If Francesca has already killed or harmed enormous numbers of people, the situation is different.

Stopping the retaliation would not undo what has happened. More importantly, there are now living people who could remember her as an unattractive person responsible for mass atrocities. Future generations might preserve an image of her as the unattractive murderer who devastated humanity.

This is precisely the kind of identity she does not want to leave behind. Her concern is not fundamentally about remorse for the victims, nor about preserving moral innocence. She fears the prospect of being known or remembered in a way that combines an appearance she despises with a public identity she finds intolerable.

At this point, she may abandon the belief that human suffering can hurt God **while retaining a powerful reason, by her own priorities, to eliminate the remaining human population**.

The objective changes:

- **Before God's clarification:** Cause human suffering in the hope that it hurts God.
- **After God's clarification, if enough harm has already occurred:** Prevent anyone in surviving humanity from remembering, witnessing, or later transmitting the unwanted identity she has acquired.

She continues toward the eradication of humanity, not because she still believes the original revenge theory, and not merely because she has already invested effort in it, but because **the atrocities have created a new reputational threat**.

This is not accurately explained by the sunk-cost fallacy alone. The previous harm creates circumstances that she now considers undesirable, and removing witnesses becomes a new instrumental goal. Her reasoning remains morally monstrous, but its proximate purpose is no longer the same as before.

The distinction does not imply a fixed numerical threshold, such as exactly half the population. The deciding factor is when Francesca regards the existence of surviving observers and a surviving historical memory as intolerable. The example of being 'halfway through' illustrates that the campaign has progressed far enough to change her perceived incentives, not that she performs a literal percentage calculation.

In this later branch, after she believes she has eliminated the human observers capable of preserving that memory, she ultimately ends her own life. She accepts the threatened consequence of hell rather than choosing to live publicly as the person she has been forced to become.

### 9.3 The scope of the erasure objective

In this scenario, Francesca's immediate aim is to leave **no surviving human consciousness able to know or remember her as unattractive**. The broader phrasing 'no consciousness left on Earth' requires care: human extinction would not literally erase every conscious animal, any nonhuman intelligent society, the hypothetical God, or possible records accessible to observers outside Earth.

The established intention here is to destroy humanity and its capacity to preserve her unwanted reputation. Whether she would target additional kinds of conscious observers depends on whether she believes they could recognize and perpetuate the same information. This distinction should not be silently resolved by treating *humanity* and *all possible consciousness* as interchangeable.

Similarly, killing all current human observers would not logically erase knowledge from an omniscient God. Francesca's objective is focused on the human audience she no longer wants to exist, not a proven ability to remove all information about herself from reality.

## 10. What changes in her mental state, and what does not

**What can change in the hypothetical:**

- She receives conclusive information that an omnipotent God exists and has imposed an unavoidable restriction.
- Her physical appearance and possibilities for altering it are changed by external intervention.
- She becomes distressed, frustrated, and eventually intensely hateful toward God.
- Human suffering, ordinarily emotionally irrelevant to her, becomes instrumentally desirable as a possible method of revenge.
- If that method is conclusively ruled out after large-scale crimes, eliminating survivors becomes instrumentally desirable for reputation management instead.
- Her final decisions depend on information, timing, and the consequences of her previous actions.

**What does not change in her underlying psychology:**

- Beauty remains her highest personal priority.
- Genuine attachments remain possible but subordinate to beauty.
- She can distinguish what she wants from what she can justify as objectively true or morally correct.
- She is capable of extraordinary intellectual understanding without corresponding compassion.
- She can recognize uncertainty, revise factual beliefs, and abandon a plan shown to be ineffective.
- She does not intrinsically value preventing another person's suffering.
- She does not need to enjoy suffering for its own sake to deliberately inflict it.
- Her emotions can be powerful without rendering her incapable of deliberate choices.

The appearance of hatred, an active desire to inflict suffering, or an obsession with eliminating witnesses is therefore **a situational change in immediate motivation, not a replacement of Francesca's foundational values**.

There is also no implication that her acts are involuntary. She may feel cornered and unable to restore her beauty, but the terrible things she chooses to do to other people remain choices. An impossible personal goal does not make harming innocent people necessary or justified.

## 11. The comparison with AM: similar outcomes, different targets of hatred

This thought experiment can produce behavior outwardly reminiscent of **AM from *I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream***, but the source of the desire is different.

AM's hatred is principally directed toward humanity, and human torment expresses that hatred. Francesca's hatred in this hypothetical is directed at **God**, not intrinsically at human beings. She uses humans as a possible means of affecting him.

As a result, both may actively seek extraordinary human suffering, but they are not necessarily motivated by the same psychological reward. Francesca could feel victorious if she thought God was distressed, even while remaining indifferent to the victims' experience considered on its own.

And when she becomes convinced that suffering cannot hurt God, that aim can disappear. The later attempt to eliminate humanity, if she is already far into the campaign, is a new **reputation-erasure** objective. This again illustrates that extreme cruelty can arise from more than one motivation without Francesca acquiring a permanent, intrinsic love of cruelty.

Whether this makes her 'more evil' or 'less evil' than AM depends on what one means by evil: hatred, sadistic enjoyment, indifference, willingness to harm, or the values governing decisions. The hypothetical establishes her capacity for extreme deliberate malevolence, not a single objective ranking against every other fictional character.

## 12. Why this does not contradict her ordinary behavior

A person can lack an active desire to torture others in ordinary life and nevertheless develop such a desire when they see torture as a way to satisfy an intense existing motive. Francesca's indifference to suffering never guaranteed that she would always be passive about it. It only meant that suffering, in itself, did not normally attract or repel her.

In the divine-coercion hypothetical, suffering first becomes useful to revenge. After the supposed revenge loses its purpose, killing survivors can become useful to preventing an unwanted reputation. In both cases, the outcomes depend on **what Francesca wants**, not on any change in the intrinsic value she assigns to other people's lives.

Likewise, her acknowledgment that God is truly omnipotent is not intellectual submission to the fairness of his demand. She may recognize that the divine restriction is real and impossible to defeat while hating it, regarding it as a personal attack, and refusing emotionally to embrace the outcome.

The scenario should not be described as a transformation into a 'new, evil Francesca.' Nor should it be cited as evidence that she secretly wants genocide in ordinary circumstances. The same person can produce drastically different behavior when the available choices, emotional stakes, and practical incentives are drastically different.

## 13. Canonical-status and interpretation notes

1. **Not canon:** God does not appear, impose this choice, alter Francesca's body, or provoke this sequence in the story. The scenario is not foreshadowing or a planned plot arc.
2. **Same character:** All reactions are intended to follow from the already-established Francesca. This is not an alternative personality and does not overwrite her documented psychology.
3. **Conditional rather than inevitable in unrelated situations:** The specified outcomes depend on all the unusually restrictive premises being present; they should not be generalized to situations in which she can restore her beauty, conceal herself, negotiate, or pursue other preferred outcomes.
4. **God as a hypothetical device:** The use of the Christian God does not imply that Christianity exists in the series or that the depicted demand corresponds to Christian doctrine. The certainty of divine identity, the closing of loopholes, and the nonintervention during retaliation are stipulated for analytical purposes.
5. **No automatic certainty about God's feelings:** Her original belief that harming God's children might hurt God is a hypothesis she knowingly tests, not a conclusion she thinks has been proved.
6. **Two possible responses to conclusive failure:** Before the campaign, she abandons it and ultimately chooses death under the scenario's conditions. After extensive atrocities, she may continue toward human extinction to eliminate surviving witnesses to her unwanted identity. These branches must not be conflated.
7. **No implied sadism:** Deliberately maximizing suffering can be instrumental to revenge without involving pleasure in the victims' suffering for its own sake.
8. **No moral excuse:** Explaining how her reasoning produces atrocities does not justify them or reduce the victims to mere details of her inner conflict.

## 14. Central psychological conclusion

**This hypothetical does not change who Francesca Badu is. It changes what happens to her and therefore what her existing psychology leads her to want and do.**

Her supreme desire for beauty remains in place. Her ability to understand reality remains in place. Her willingness to acknowledge uncertainty remains in place. Her profound indifference to other people's suffering remains in place. Her capacity for powerful emotional responses remains in place.

An omnipotent being takes away the thing she values above everything else, refuses every possible workaround, and cannot be defeated. Francesca's hatred is therefore directed at that being. She considers whether the being cares about humanity in a way that can be exploited, and she is willing to impose terrible suffering on innocent people to test that possibility. She does not need to hate the victims or enjoy their pain.

If she learns early that this retaliation is futile, she does not begin it. If she learns only after committing massive atrocities, her goals can shift toward eliminating the remaining human audience that might remember her as unattractive and infamous. The aim of her conduct changes; the underlying hierarchy of her values does not.

**The fact that the actual story never places Francesca in this situation does not mean she lacks the psychological capacity to respond this way. It means that the story never supplies the conditions that would reveal this particular response.**
